Introduction to Physical AI

Learning Objectives​

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  • Define Physical AI and contrast it with purely digital AI systems
  • Explain the concept of embodied intelligence and why a physical body shapes cognition
  • Survey the current humanoid robotics landscape and identify major platforms
  • Describe the core sensor modalities used in humanoid robots: LiDAR, cameras, IMUs, and force/torque sensors

1.1 What is Physical AI?​

Ask a large language model to clean your kitchen. It can generate a detailed plan — clear the counter, load the dishwasher, wipe surfaces — but it cannot execute a single step. The model has no arms, no eyes, no sense of how heavy a plate is or how slippery a wet floor feels. It exists entirely in the space of tokens and probabilities.

Physical AI bridges this gap. It refers to AI systems that operate in and interact with the real, physical world through embodied agents — primarily robots.

Core Definition

Physical AI = Perception + Reasoning + Physical Action. An AI system that senses the environment, reasons about physical constraints (gravity, friction, collisions), and acts through motors, grippers, or legs.

The Three Pillars of Physical AI​

PillarDigital AIPhysical AI
PerceptionText, images, structured dataCameras, LiDAR, IMUs, force sensors — noisy, continuous, real-time
ReasoningLanguage understanding, classification, generationSpatial reasoning, physics prediction, motion planning
ActionGenerate text, API calls, file writesMove joints, grip objects, walk, balance

The critical difference is the closed loop: a Physical AI system acts, observes the result of its action, and adjusts. When a robot reaches for a cup and the cup slides, it must detect the slip in milliseconds and correct its grasp. There is no "undo" button in the physical world.

From Digital AI to Physical AI​

The progression from digital to physical AI follows a clear trajectory:

Digital AI          Embodied AI           Physical AI
(Pure Software)     (Sim + Hardware)      (Real-World Deployment)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
GPT, BERT,          Simulated robots,     Warehouse robots,
image classifiers   digital twins         humanoid assistants
                                          surgical robots

Key Addition:       Key Addition:         Key Addition:
  None                Physics engine        Real physics,
                      Simulated sensors     Real sensors,
                      Virtual actuators     Real actuators

Each step adds constraints. A simulated robot can be reset after a fall; a physical robot risks damaged hardware. A simulated camera returns perfect depth maps; a real camera has lens distortion, motion blur, and lighting changes. These constraints are not bugs — they are the defining feature of Physical AI.

Why Physical AI Matters Now​

Several converging trends make Physical AI practical today:

  1. Foundation models for robotics: Models trained on internet-scale data can now provide semantic understanding (recognizing objects, understanding instructions) that previously required hand-crafted perception pipelines.

  2. Sim-to-real transfer: Training policies in simulation and deploying on real hardware has matured dramatically, reducing the need for expensive real-world data collection.

  3. Hardware cost reduction: Actuators, sensors, and compute boards have dropped in price while improving in capability.

  4. ROS 2 maturity: The middleware ecosystem (which you will master in this module) now provides production-grade communication, real-time guarantees, and a massive library of packages.

1.2 Embodied Intelligence​

A brain without a body is a fundamentally different kind of intelligence than a brain with one. This insight, central to the field of embodied cognition, has profound implications for robotics.

The Embodiment Hypothesis​

The embodiment hypothesis states that intelligence is not just a property of the brain (or the neural network) but arises from the dynamic interaction between brain, body, and environment. Consider how a human infant learns to grasp:

  1. The infant reaches randomly, sometimes contacting objects
  2. Contact produces tactile feedback — texture, temperature, resistance
  3. The visual system correlates what it sees with what it feels
  4. Over thousands of attempts, a coordinated reach-and-grasp emerges

The body is not just executing commands from the brain; the body's physical properties (finger length, joint flexibility, skin sensitivity) shape what the brain learns. A robot with rigid parallel-jaw grippers learns a fundamentally different grasping strategy than one with soft, compliant fingers.

Morphological Computation​

Some computation is performed by the body itself, not the brain. This is called morphological computation:

  • Passive dynamics in walking: A well-designed leg with the right mass distribution can walk down a slope with zero motor input. The physics of the pendulum does the computation.
  • Compliant grasping: A soft gripper conforms to objects automatically. The brain sends "close gripper" and the material properties handle the rest.
  • Whisker sensing: A flexible whisker bends on contact, and the bend pattern directly encodes the shape of the contacted object.
# Conceptual example: How embodiment changes the control problem

# Without embodiment - the controller must handle everything
class DisembodiedController:
    def grasp(self, object_shape, object_weight, friction_coeff):
        # Must compute exact finger positions
        # Must compute exact grip force
        # Must model contact mechanics
        # Must handle every edge case in software
        force = self.compute_exact_grip_force(object_weight, friction_coeff)
        positions = self.compute_finger_positions(object_shape)
        return GraspCommand(force=force, positions=positions)

# With embodiment - compliant hardware simplifies the problem
class EmbodiedController:
    def grasp(self, detected_object):
        # Soft fingers conform to shape automatically
        # Material compliance handles force distribution
        # Controller only needs "close until contact"
        return GraspCommand(action="close", speed="slow")

Intelligence Through Interaction​

Physical AI must reason about affordances — what actions the environment permits. A table affords placing objects on it. A door handle affords pulling or pushing. A staircase affords climbing. These affordances are defined by the relationship between the robot's body and the environment, not by either one alone.

Key Insight

Digital AI processes information. Physical AI processes information and physics. Every decision must account for gravity, friction, inertia, compliance, and the irreversibility of physical actions.

1.3 The Humanoid Robotics Landscape​

Why build robots that look like humans? The answer is pragmatic: the world is designed for the human body. Door handles are at human hand height. Stairs are sized for human legs. Tools are shaped for human grippers. A humanoid form factor gives a robot immediate access to the built environment without modification.

Degrees of Freedom​

A humanoid robot's complexity is often measured in degrees of freedom (DoF) — the number of independent joint motions. The human body has approximately 244 DoF (most in the hands). Current humanoid robots typically have:

  • Legs: 6 DoF per leg (hip: 3, knee: 1, ankle: 2) = 12 DoF
  • Arms: 7 DoF per arm (shoulder: 3, elbow: 1, wrist: 3) = 14 DoF
  • Torso: 2-3 DoF (waist rotation, flexion)
  • Hands: 6-20 DoF per hand (depending on dexterity)
  • Head/Neck: 2-3 DoF (pan, tilt)

A typical full-body humanoid has 30 to 50+ DoF, each requiring coordinated control — a control problem that ROS 2 helps solve through its distributed node architecture.

The Control Challenge​

Controlling a humanoid robot is fundamentally a real-time, multi-objective optimization problem:

# Simplified humanoid control loop (conceptual)
import numpy as np

class HumanoidController:
    """
    A humanoid must simultaneously:
    1. Maintain balance (center of mass over support polygon)
    2. Track desired trajectories (arm reaching, walking)
    3. Avoid collisions (self-collision and environment)
    4. Respect joint limits and torque limits
    5. React to disturbances (someone bumps the robot)
    """

    def __init__(self, num_joints: int = 40):
        self.num_joints = num_joints
        self.joint_positions = np.zeros(num_joints)
        self.joint_velocities = np.zeros(num_joints)

    def compute_control(self, desired_state, sensor_readings):
        # Balance controller (highest priority)
        com_position = self.compute_center_of_mass()
        support_polygon = self.compute_support_polygon(sensor_readings.foot_contacts)
        balance_torques = self.balance_controller(com_position, support_polygon)

        # Task controller (track desired motion)
        task_torques = self.task_controller(desired_state)

        # Combine with priority: balance > task > posture
        # This is solved as a quadratic program in practice
        return self.prioritized_control(balance_torques, task_torques)

    def control_loop(self):
        """Must run at 1000 Hz for stable bipedal walking."""
        CONTROL_RATE_HZ = 1000
        dt = 1.0 / CONTROL_RATE_HZ
        # In ROS 2, this would be a timer callback in a node
        pass

This is why we need ROS 2: it provides the real-time communication infrastructure that lets perception nodes, planning nodes, and control nodes work together at the speeds that bipedal locomotion demands.

1.4 Sensor Systems for Physical AI​

A robot without sensors is blind and deaf. Physical AI requires rich, multi-modal sensing to build an internal model of the world. Here we cover the four sensor families most critical to humanoid robotics.

Cameras and Vision Systems​

Cameras are the highest-bandwidth sensors on most robots. Modern humanoid robots use several camera types:

RGB Cameras provide color images for object recognition, face detection, and scene understanding. Typical configurations include stereo pairs for depth estimation.

Depth Cameras (Intel RealSense, Azure Kinect) provide per-pixel distance measurements using structured light or time-of-flight. They produce point clouds — 3D representations of the visible scene.

Event Cameras detect per-pixel brightness changes asynchronously, providing microsecond-level temporal resolution for fast motion tracking.

# Example: Processing a depth image to create a point cloud
import numpy as np

def depth_to_point_cloud(depth_image, camera_intrinsics):
    """
    Convert a depth image to a 3D point cloud.

    Args:
        depth_image: HxW array of depth values in meters
        camera_intrinsics: dict with 'fx', 'fy', 'cx', 'cy'

    Returns:
        Nx3 array of 3D points
    """
    height, width = depth_image.shape
    fx = camera_intrinsics['fx']
    fy = camera_intrinsics['fy']
    cx = camera_intrinsics['cx']
    cy = camera_intrinsics['cy']

    # Create pixel coordinate grids
    u = np.arange(width)
    v = np.arange(height)
    u, v = np.meshgrid(u, v)

    # Back-project to 3D
    z = depth_image
    x = (u - cx) * z / fx
    y = (v - cy) * z / fy

    # Stack into Nx3 array, removing invalid (zero-depth) points
    points = np.stack([x, y, z], axis=-1).reshape(-1, 3)
    valid = points[:, 2] > 0
    return points[valid]

LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging)​

LiDAR sensors emit laser pulses and measure the time for each pulse to return after hitting a surface. This produces highly accurate distance measurements (millimeter precision) over ranges of 10 to 100+ meters.

LiDAR TypeHow It WorksTypical Use
Spinning (Velodyne, Ouster)Rotating laser array, 360-degree coverageOutdoor navigation, mapping
Solid-state (Livox, Hesai)No moving parts, limited FoVAutomotive, cost-sensitive
FlashIlluminates entire scene at onceShort-range 3D capture

For humanoid robots, LiDAR is particularly valuable for:

  • SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping): Building a map while tracking the robot's position
  • Obstacle detection: Identifying barriers during navigation
  • Terrain assessment: Evaluating ground surfaces before stepping

IMUs (Inertial Measurement Units)​

An IMU combines accelerometers (measuring linear acceleration) and gyroscopes (measuring angular velocity) to track orientation and movement. Most include a magnetometer for absolute heading reference.

For bipedal robots, the IMU is mission-critical. It provides:

  • Body orientation: Is the robot tilting? How fast?
  • Impact detection: Did the foot just hit the ground?
  • Slip detection: Is the base accelerating unexpectedly?
# Example: Simple complementary filter for IMU orientation estimation
import numpy as np

class ComplementaryFilter:
    """
    Fuses gyroscope and accelerometer data for orientation estimation.
    The gyroscope is accurate short-term but drifts.
    The accelerometer gives absolute gravity direction but is noisy.
    """

    def __init__(self, alpha=0.98):
        self.alpha = alpha  # Weight for gyroscope (trust short-term)
        self.pitch = 0.0
        self.roll = 0.0

    def update(self, gyro_rates, accel_data, dt):
        """
        Args:
            gyro_rates: [gx, gy, gz] in rad/s
            accel_data: [ax, ay, az] in m/s^2
            dt: time step in seconds
        """
        # Integrate gyroscope (fast, but drifts)
        gyro_pitch = self.pitch + gyro_rates[1] * dt
        gyro_roll = self.roll + gyro_rates[0] * dt

        # Compute angles from accelerometer (noisy, but no drift)
        accel_pitch = np.arctan2(accel_data[0],
                                  np.sqrt(accel_data[1]**2 + accel_data[2]**2))
        accel_roll = np.arctan2(accel_data[1],
                                 np.sqrt(accel_data[0]**2 + accel_data[2]**2))

        # Fuse: trust gyro short-term, accel long-term
        self.pitch = self.alpha * gyro_pitch + (1 - self.alpha) * accel_pitch
        self.roll = self.alpha * gyro_roll + (1 - self.alpha) * accel_roll

        return self.pitch, self.roll

Force/Torque Sensors​

Force/torque (F/T) sensors measure the forces and torques applied at a specific point — typically at the wrist (for manipulation) and at the feet (for locomotion).

Wrist F/T sensors enable:

  • Detecting contact with objects
  • Measuring grip force to prevent crushing or dropping
  • Impedance control (making the arm behave like a spring)

Foot F/T sensors enable:

  • Detecting ground contact during walking
  • Measuring the Center of Pressure (CoP) — critical for balance
  • Estimating ground reaction forces for dynamic locomotion
Sensor Fusion is Essential

No single sensor provides a complete picture. Physical AI systems must fuse data from all sensor modalities — combining camera semantics ("that is a table"), LiDAR geometry ("the table surface is 0.75m high"), IMU dynamics ("I am leaning 2 degrees forward"), and F/T feedback ("my hand is pressing with 5N of force"). Sensor fusion is a theme that will recur throughout this course.

Sensor Data Rates and the Need for Middleware​

Different sensors produce data at vastly different rates:

SensorTypical RateData Size per Message
IMU200-1000 Hz~100 bytes
Force/Torque100-1000 Hz~50 bytes
Joint encoders1000 Hz~200 bytes
LiDAR10-20 Hz~1 MB
RGB Camera30-60 Hz~2 MB
Depth Camera30 Hz~1 MB

A humanoid robot with full sensing produces hundreds of megabytes per second of data that must be routed to the right processing nodes with the right timing guarantees. This is exactly the problem that ROS 2 solves — and why it is the subject of the rest of this module.

1.5 Summary​

Physical AI extends artificial intelligence from the digital realm into the physical world. Unlike digital AI, Physical AI systems must:

  • Close the perception-action loop in real time
  • Reason about physics: gravity, friction, inertia, contact
  • Handle noisy, continuous sensor streams from multiple modalities
  • Coordinate dozens of actuators simultaneously

Humanoid robots represent the most complex Physical AI challenge — and ROS 2 provides the middleware backbone that makes this coordination possible. In the next chapter, we dive into the ROS 2 architecture.
